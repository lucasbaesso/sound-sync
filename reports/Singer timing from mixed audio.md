# Anchor the backing before timing the singer

No published method recovers a constant vocal-path latency from a single mixed mono track, and timing evidence alone can only partly identify it. What a mix reveals is the **sum of the technical latency L and the singer's own mean timing bias b**, and only modulo the aliases of the metrical grid. That sum can be measured with roughly **±5–15 ms of random error** from 60–150 vocal landmarks per song. The unknown singer bias (plausibly −60 to +40 ms, later for R&B-style phrasing) caps absolute accuracy at roughly **±40–60 ms**. So an extension can confidently flag offsets above about 120 ms, but it cannot tell a 60 ms latency from a laid-back singer. At 80 BPM the larger practical danger is aliasing. A 16th note lasts 187.5 ms, so on any rhythm-only statistic a **200 ms latency looks like a 12.5 ms "on time" singer** and a 250 ms latency looks like 62.5 ms. When the backing recording can be identified, the strongest approach is reference-anchored: fingerprint and cross-correlate the known instrumental to pin the backing in time, mask it out, and compare the singer's words with their expected positions. That removes both separation bleed and aliasing. Without a reference, the best approach drops stem-versus-stem correlation entirely. It measures pitched vocal phrase entries against a beat/downbeat grid computed only from the drums and bass stems, scores them with an alias-aware Bayesian estimator, and aggregates across songs at different tempi, because L stays fixed while the aliases move. Both pipelines are computationally feasible in Chrome today. Before the team's existing remix test can validate any of them, it needs one fix: remixing *separated* stems plants a zero-lag copy of the voice in the test signal itself.

## A single mix only reveals latency plus singer bias, modulo the beat

The latency the extension wants to find comes from the streamer's setup, not from the stream. The singer hears the backing through a playback path (d_out) and is captured through a microphone path (d_in). The stream mixes the backing as sent with the voice as captured. Singers and players lock to what they hear. In networked-performance studies, one-way delays above about **14 ms make ensembles slow down**, and synchronization stays workable only up to about **20–30 ms** ([Chafe et al., CCRMA](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~cc/pub/html/ntd.html); [Rottondi et al. 2016](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2723336/1/nmp.pdf)). A singer who aligns to a backing that arrives late by d_out therefore produces a voice that lands roughly d_out + d_in late in the mix. The singer cannot hear d_in at all, so they cannot correct for it. No source found shows singers automatically pre-compensating for such a constant offset. Consumer round-trips of this size are common: BandLab users report **100–200 ms** from its click-based latency test ([BandLab FAQ](https://blog.bandlab.com/studio-faq/)). One useful simplification follows. Every delay that happens *after* the mix (AAC/Opus encoder lookahead, CDN, player buffer) shifts voice and backing together and cancels out. Codecs matter only through onset smearing, and no source quantified that.

Write each observed vocal landmark as o_i = L + b + e_i, where b is the singer's mean bias (anticipation, style, and the onset detector's own convention) and e_i is per-note scatter. Precision is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is how much is known about b, and the literature constrains it only loosely. Finger tapping runs **20–80 ms ahead of the beat** ([Repp & Su 2013 record](https://www.altmetric.com/details/1262980)). Vocal responses are hypothesised to anticipate less, because voice and pacer share the auditory modality ([Konstanz KOPS](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/783996b2-6c04-4316-8b36-5bc2f13d64b3/content)). In singing duos the median signed asynchrony is only **−4 ms**, but the mean absolute asynchrony is **59 ms** and the SD is **67 ms** ([D'Amario et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01208/full)). Across 88,357 commercial vocal tracks, singers slightly anticipate beats 1 and 3, by −0.012 in the paper's normalized units. That is about 9 ms early at 80 BPM if the unit is a beat, or about 25 ms early at 120 BPM if it is a bar; the paper is ambiguous. R&B, Latin and Metal sit about a **32nd note late**, which is roughly **94 ms at 80 BPM** ([Georgieva et al., TISMIR 2026](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)). Jazz soloists delay downbeats by about 30 ms and up to about 100 ms ([KTH Ensemble Swing](https://www.speech.kth.se/music/performance/Texts/ensemble_swing.htm)). Drummers told to play "laid back" delay by only **17.4 ms** ([Câmara, UiO](https://duo.uio.no/bitstream/handle/10852/79965/MP3801_01_Camara.pdf?sequence=1)). A defensible prior is b ~ N(−10 ms, 25–30 ms), with a tail to about +95 ms for R&B-style singers at ballad tempo. Under that prior, an estimate of L + b above about 120 ms is almost certainly technical. From 50 to 100 ms, latency and style are genuinely confounded.

Three refinements sharpen this limit without removing it. First, the onset definition adds its own bias. Listeners and singers place a syllable's beat near the **vowel onset (P-centre)**, not the consonant burst, so an energy-onset detector and a pitch-onset detector disagree by tens of milliseconds depending on the phonemes sung ([Barbosa et al. 2005](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2005/barbosa05_interspeech.pdf); [Rathcke et al.](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/how-to-hit-that-beat-testing-acoustic-anchors-of-rhythmic-movemen/)). That bias can be calibrated; the singer's bias cannot. Second, structure helps only a little. Expressive timing depends on beat position (beats 1/3 vs 2/4 in pop; downbeats vs upbeats in jazz), whereas latency shifts every position equally. But the position differences reported are about **10 ms**, far too small to anchor a 50–300 ms estimate ([TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)). Third, a **singer's persistent personal bias is permanently confounded with L**. No single-mix method separates it, and a reference recording does not either: a reference shows how the original artist timed the song, not where "correct" lies.

### Metrical aliasing turns 200 ms of latency into "on time"

Any estimate built from vocal timing against a beat grid has a likelihood that is periodic in every metrical level that carries onset mass. At ballad tempo, those periods fall inside the search range:

| 80 BPM level | Period | True L = 150 ms looks like | True L = 200 ms looks like | True L = 250 ms looks like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 16th note | 187.5 ms | −37.5 ms | **+12.5 ms** | +62.5 ms |
| 8th-note triplet (12/8 feel) | 250 ms | −100 ms | −50 ms | **0 ms** |
| 8th note | 375 ms | −225 ms | −175 ms | −125 ms |
| Beat (double-tempo tracker error) | 375 ms | same as 8th | same as 8th | same as 8th |

A prior that favours small |L + b| is the natural way to choose among aliases, and it picks the **wrong** one exactly when latency is largest. Beat trackers make this worse on ballads. On slow, expressive material the state of the art degrades sharply (Beat This! scores 62.7 F1 on SMC versus 89.1 on GTZAN) ([Foscarin et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21658)), and octave errors are a principal failure mode ([Ahn et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12287)). A double-tempo grid at 80 BPM halves the beat period to 375 ms and moves its 16th alias to 94 ms. Rhythm-only evidence therefore needs landmarks with a long period (phrase entries landing on downbeats, with a 3 s bar at 80 BPM 4/4) or a non-rhythmic tie-breaker.

For a sense of scale, listeners detect asynchrony differences somewhere **between 10 and 38 ms** in sung ensembles ([D'Amario et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561579)). An estimator good to ±40–60 ms can therefore *flag* a problem but cannot *correct* it to inaudibility. That should shape what the product promises.

## Separation bleed pins every stem-versus-stem comparison at zero

The team's four dead ends share one cause. Model the separator's output as vocal stem = V(t−L) + a·A(t) and accompaniment stem = A(t) + c·V(t−L). The cross-correlation of any feature of the two stems then contains a·(A⋆A) and c·(V⋆V), and both terms peak at **lag 0** whatever L is. The genuine cross term V⋆A is weak, because voice and backing onsets are different events that share only the beat. Where that term does have structure, it is aliased by the beat. Bleed is a documented property of Demucs-family separators, which "suffer from some bleeding, especially between the vocals and other source" ([Défossez et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13254)). It is reduced but not eliminated in Hybrid Demucs ([arXiv 2111.03600](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.03600)). Nothing about it is specific to MDX-Net. Onset envelopes, chroma novelty, autocorrelation-shape subtraction and cepstral echo search all inherit the same zero-lag term or the same beat aliases. A further variant, regressing one stem out of the other at lag 0 before correlating, is mathematically close to the subtraction already tried, so it deserves low priority. Rescoring remixes over candidate shifts also favours zero when the stems carry bleed.

The validation experiment needs fixing too. When separated stems are remixed with the voice shifted by d and then re-separated, the test signal contains A + c·V(t) + V(t−d) + a·A(t−d). It holds a **vocal copy at zero lag**, which arrives through the accompaniment stem's bleed, and a backing echo at +d. The re-separated vocal stem then carries voice energy at both 0 and d. Part of the "pinned at 0 ms" result was therefore planted by the test itself. A sound validation needs genuinely isolated stems, such as multitrack sessions or karaoke captures recorded on separate channels. Separated stems are acceptable only if their cross-bleed is measured and reported alongside the result.

What survives this analysis is a design rule: **never compare the two stems with each other**. Instead, compare each stem with something the other cannot contaminate. The drums and bass stems carry little vocal energy, so they can define the grid. Pitched voicing onsets carry little drum bleed, because drums are unpitched. And when a reference recording is available, it is independent of the stream by construction. Both recommended pipelines below follow this rule.

## Five candidates ranked by accuracy and feasibility

The ranking weighs expected accuracy at 80 BPM against what the extension can realistically obtain. Error figures are synthesized estimates built from the cited component numbers, not published end-to-end results, because no paper measures this task directly.

| Rank | Approach | Needs | Random error per song | Bias floor | Alias risk | Browser feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reference-anchored, identical backing recording | Version-exact fingerprint library; lyrics or reference vocal | ~1 ms on backing; ~10–20 ms on vocal | Singer vs original artist, ±40–60 ms | None (event identity known) | Compute easy; coverage limited by library |
| 2 | Reference-anchored via DTW, different version of same song | Studio recording | ~20–50 ms per event before averaging | Same, plus vocal-dominance bias in DTW | Low | Medium |
| 3 | Independent-grid phase estimator with alias breakers, aggregated across songs | Nothing external | ~5–15 ms | Singer prior ±40–60 ms; detector/tracker bias, calibratable | High per song at 80 BPM; reduced by phrase entries and cross-tempo aggregation | High |
| 4 | Lyrics forced alignment against the independent grid | Lyrics text | 40–100 ms median per word; ~10 ms after averaging | Aligner signed bias unknown | Medium | Medium |
| 5 | Harmonic-consistency tie-breaker | Nothing | Not a ms estimator | — | Resolves 16th/8th aliases only | High |

Stem-versus-stem correlation of any feature, lag-0 orthogonalization, and remix-and-rescore are not worth further effort, for the reasons above. Multichannel time-delay estimation (DUET, DEMIX, GCC-PHAT between channels) needs at least two channels and does not apply to mono ([Arberet et al., EPFL](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/150461/)).

### Rank 1: lock the backing to a reference, then time the words

When the stream's backing is a known karaoke or instrumental recording, the problem largely dissolves. Landmark fingerprinting identifies tracks "in the presence of voices… and even other music". It reaches **50% recognition at −9/−6/−3 dB SNR** for 15/10/5 s excerpts, and it returns the query's time offset within the reference as a by-product ([Wang 2003](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/Wang03-shazam.pdf)). The catch is that it is strictly version-specific, so the library must hold the exact karaoke master. Panako is an open-source implementation that also tolerates ~10% time-scale and pitch changes ([Six & Leman](https://github.com/JorenSix/Panako)). AcoustID cannot serve here: it matches whole files only, and its free API is non-commercial ([acoustid.org](https://acoustid.org/webservice)).

The concrete pipeline has five steps. First, fingerprint 10–15 s of the stream server-side to identify the backing and get a coarse offset. Second, refine the offset with GCC-PHAT between the reference instrumental and the stream, or its separated accompaniment stem, over windows of 10 s or more, treating the live voice as uncorrelated noise. A 2025 K-pop pipeline uses exactly this GCC-PHAT stage (±20 s search, then ±0.25 s frame-wise refinement) and warns that "even the slightest tempo mismatch would nullify the effectiveness" ([arXiv 2508.20273](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20273v1)), so check for drift between windows. Third, build a reference-informed Wiener mask from the aligned instrumental rather than subtracting waveforms. Codec, EQ and mastering mismatches leave residue that is time-locked to the backing, and that residue would pull vocal onsets back toward zero. Fourth, run the same CTC forced aligner on the masked live vocal and on the reference vocal (the original studio vocal, or the karaoke guide vocal) placed on the instrumental's timeline. Running one aligner on both cancels its onset-definition bias to first order. Fifth, take the median of per-word differences.

State-of-the-art lyrics alignment on polyphonic mixes has median word errors of **0.04–0.10 s** ([MIREX 2020](https://music-ir.org/mirex/wiki/2020:Automatic_Lyrics-to-Audio_Alignment_Results)). Over about 100 words, the median of the differences should settle within roughly 10–20 ms. Because every event's identity is known, aliasing disappears. The bias floor does not: the result is still L plus the singer's deviation from the original artist. This is the same logic as Sony's SingStar patent, which correlates detected notes against target notes over **±3 note-clock periods**, but that system *absorbs* the best-matching offset rather than reporting it ([US8634759](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8634759)).

Rank 2 relaxes the version requirement. Align the stream's accompaniment stem to the original recording's accompaniment with multiscale DTW on chroma plus onset features ([Sync Toolbox](https://github.com/meinardmueller/synctoolbox)). Expect errors of tens of ms: MATCH reports a **41 ms mean and 20 ms median** on piano ([MATCH](https://www.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/~simond/match/)), and accuracy is worse where the singing voice dominates ([Özer et al. 2021](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2021/latebreaking/000025.pdf)).

### Rank 3: an independent grid, pitched landmarks, and an alias-aware posterior

For streams with no reference, the most promising design follows the bleed-avoidance rule at every stage. It mirrors the pipeline already validated at scale for microtiming: HTDemucs vocals → onset detection → Beat This! grid ([TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)). The changes are that the grid is built from *non-vocal* stems and the vocal landmarks are chosen to resist bleed.

Run HTDemucs four-stem separation, duplicating the mono input into both channels. Compute the grid with Beat This! on drums + bass + other only. Do not use the mixture, and do not use All-In-One, whose demixed input includes the vocal stem and would let the voice pull the grid ([Kim & Nam 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.16425)). Constrain tempo to a ballad range, test the half- and double-tempo hypotheses explicitly, and refine each beat's phase against percussive onsets on the drums or bass stem. Beat This! runs at 20 ms frames and is trained with ±70 ms slop, and no tracker reports its signed bias in ms ([Foscarin et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21658)), so this refinement step matters.

For landmarks, track f0 on the vocal stem (SwiftF0 or RMVPE) and keep **voicing onsets that follow at least 250 ms of unvoiced vocal stem**. These are phrase and word entries. Weight each landmark by pitch confidence and by vocal-stem energy above the local bleed floor. Do not reject onsets just because they coincide with drum hits: that selects against the zero-lag hypothesis and biases the estimate away from it. Keep energy (SuperFlux) onsets as a second landmark class with its own bias term. Their typical F ≈ 0.74 at ±50 ms on separated vocals ([TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)) makes them noisier, and the gap between the two classes measures the P-centre offset.

The estimator is a grid search over L from −100 to +400 ms in 1 ms steps. For each candidate L, score each landmark's residual against the nearest metrical position with a Student-t mixture. Weight positions by metrical level (downbeat > beat > 8th > 16th), and give phrase entries extra weight at the bar level, where the period is 3 s. Add a uniform outlier component, since about a quarter of onsets on mixtures are spurious or missed ([arXiv 2306.12714](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.12714)). Assuming normally distributed asynchronies underestimates both mean and spread, so robust likelihoods are warranted ([Behav. Res. Methods](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13428-015-0591-2)). Multiply by the prior on b. Report the MAP estimate, a 95% credible interval, and the **mass ratio between the top two modes** as an explicit alias-confidence number.

With per-landmark scatter of 60–80 ms, matching the duo data plus detector error, 100 landmarks give a standard error of about 6–8 ms, and 30 phrase entries alone give about 12–15 ms. Smule's crowd-sourced patent is the closest published analogue. It matches vocal syllable peaks with backing beats "at candidate offsets" and pools **300+ performances per device model**, because single performances correspond too loosely ([US11146901](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11146901)). That suggests a single ballad will usually yield an estimate of about ±30–50 ms, not ±10 ms.

### Breaking aliases with harmony, lyrics, video and repetition

Four tie-breakers complement the rhythm posterior. **Harmonic consistency** scores, for each surviving alias, how consonant the vocal's sustained pitch classes are with the concurrent accompaniment chroma, computed with the vocal f0 harmonics masked out. This differs from the chroma-novelty approach that failed. It does not estimate L. It only arbitrates between candidates 187.5 ms apart, and chord changes on beats or bars have much longer periods than that. It remains unvalidated. **Forced alignment of known lyrics** on the vocal stem gives word identities and syllable onsets near the P-centre. CTC aligners exist as INT8 ONNX for the browser ([charsiu-js](https://huggingface.co/mnaoizyyy/charsiu-js-models); [Fast Aligner](https://huggingface.co/RandomThingsIDo/fast-aligner)). Whisper's stock WebGPU path cannot return word timestamps ([transformers.js #820](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers.js/issues/820)). **The team's lips-vs-voice measurement** offers a bleed-free landmark source. Mouth-opening events, shifted by the measured lips-to-voice offset, mark vocal entries in audio time without any audio separation. They still carry b and the aliases, but not leakage. This is an inference to test.

The most powerful tie-breaker is **aggregation across songs**. L is a property of the streamer's rig and stays constant across a session. Aliases sit at tempo-dependent offsets: the 16th alias falls at 187.5 ms at 80 BPM, 150 ms at 100 BPM and 125 ms at 120 BPM. Multiplying per-song posteriors over L lets the true mode reinforce while the aliases spread out. It also averages away song-specific style, leaving only the singer's persistent bias. This turns Smule's device-level pooling into streamer-level pooling, which a viewer-side extension can do without anyone's cooperation.

## Validate against planted offsets before trusting any number

Every number above is an estimate, and the decisive unknown is the **alias-error rate on 80 BPM ballads**. Validation should therefore measure signed error, the absolute-error distribution, the alias-error rate (|error| > 90 ms), and credible-interval coverage. Four ground-truth sources fit together:

| Ground truth | How to build it | What it measures | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clean multitracks with planted shifts | Isolated stems mixed mono with the voice shifted by 0, ±25, 50, 100, 150, 187.5, 200, 250, 300 ms, then encoded to AAC 128k and Opus 64–96k | Estimator accuracy, alias handling, codec sensitivity; isolates algorithm error with b ≈ studio timing | Studio vocals are edited toward the grid ([TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)), so b is unrealistically small |
| Separated stems with planted shifts (current method) | Same, from MDX-Net/HTDemucs stems | Only as a stress test | Plants a zero-lag vocal copy; report the stems' cross-bleed level |
| Controlled sessions with injected monitor delay | Singers perform ballads with 0/100/200 ms added to the headphone feed, while voice and backing are recorded on separate channels | The real distribution of b for amateur singers against a backing track, which the literature lacks | Small N; needs several singers and songs |
| Live streams with lips-vs-voice | Existing video measurement | Consistency check; visual landmarks; diagnosis of the d_in side of the rig | Measures the voice-to-video path, not voice-to-backing |

The prototype should start offline in Python, in this order. Build the rank-3 pipeline and calibrate the beat-tracker and detector bias on clean multitracks, where the true offset is known. Run the alias sweep: the 187.5 ms and 250 ms planted shifts are the tests that matter. Add the harmonic and phrase-entry tie-breakers and confirm that they lower the alias rate. Then use the controlled sessions to replace the literature prior on b with a measured one. Reasonable go/no-go criteria are an alias-error rate under 5% after cross-song aggregation, and a median |error| under 25 ms on clean multitracks. If the single-song alias rate stays high, ship the product as a session accumulator, not a per-song readout. Rank 1 can be prototyped in parallel on any karaoke catalogue the team can license, since its alignment stages are simple signal processing.

The browser port fits the extension architecture. HTDemucs has an MIT-licensed WebGPU ONNX export at **126 MB**, running **~22x realtime back-to-back and ~8x when paced** to keep the UI smooth on a Radeon RX 7600. It processes fixed **7.8 s windows**, which suits an accumulate-then-estimate design ([monteslu/htdemucs-web-onnx](https://huggingface.co/monteslu/htdemucs-web-onnx)). Beat This! has an MIT ONNX export, with WebGPU about **12x faster than WASM** ([musetric/beat-this-onnx](https://huggingface.co/musetric/beat-this-onnx)), and a small variant of about 10 MB ([songbird-models](https://huggingface.co/ashudesai/songbird-models)). SwiftF0 has **95,842 parameters** under CC BY 4.0 and runs 42x faster than CREPE on CPU ([arXiv 2508.18440](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18440v1)).

Host the compute in a cross-origin-isolated offscreen document fed by `tabCapture`. Service workers are not cross-origin isolated ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/mv3/cross-origin-isolation)). Three licences need care. essentia.js is AGPL-3.0 ([MTG](https://github.com/MTG/essentia.js)). FCPE is non-commercial ([arXiv 2509.15140](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.15140v1)). The UVR MDX-Net weights currently in use have no explicit licence file ([UVR](https://github.com/Anjok07/ultimatevocalremovergui)), which is one more reason to move to HTDemucs.

## Conclusion

The question is better posed as "how large is L + b, and which alias is it?" than as "what is L?". The first part is cheap to measure. The second is where ballads defeat naive methods: at 80 BPM, the most damaging failure is not noise but a confident report of "on time" for a singer 200 ms late. The dead ends so far were not bad luck. Each correlated two signals that share a zero-lag component, and the remix test that confirmed the failure shares that component too. The two productive moves are structural: anchor to something the stream cannot contaminate (a reference recording, a drums-and-bass grid, the singer's lips), and exploit the one thing that distinguishes latency from style and alias, namely that L stays fixed across songs while tempo-dependent aliases and song-level style vary.

Even a perfect pipeline keeps a floor of roughly ±40–60 ms set by the singer's own habitual timing. No audio-only method removes it, and a reference recording only re-bases it. The honest product is therefore a three-way verdict with a credible interval: on time, ambiguous, or late by X ± 40 ms. That verdict would firm up over a session, not within a single song. If the tool ever runs on the streamer's side rather than the viewer's, a five-second speaker-to-mic pulse calibration of the kind Smule patented ([US9412390](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9412390)) would outperform everything in this report.
