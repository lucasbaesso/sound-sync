# Singer asynchrony vs. accompaniment, and whether a constant 50-300 ms latency is identifiable from timing alone

Scope note: the notes below are compiled from about 30 search and fetch calls. Several primary PDFs (Repp 2005, Repp & Su 2013, a Konstanz vocal-vs-tapping SMS paper) could not be read as text in this environment. Where a number comes only from a search-engine summary rather than a fetched primary text, it is flagged "(snippet-level)".

## Q1. Sensorimotor synchronization (SMS): negative mean asynchrony (NMA) for tapping and for singing/vocal synchronization

### Takeaway
In finger tapping to a metronome, people tap roughly 20-80 ms before the beat. Musicians show smaller NMA (closer to 0, sometimes slightly positive) and lower variability. For singing, the best quantitative data are singer-to-singer asynchronies in duos and ensembles. There the signed mean bias is close to zero (about -4 ms median), but the per-note scatter is large (mean absolute asynchrony about 50-95 ms). Single-voice NMA values for singing along with a backing track are not well documented in the sources found.

### Cited Findings
- **Tapping NMA magnitude.** In tapping to a beat, responses typically come about 20-80 ms before the beat ("negative mean asynchrony"). Trained musicians generally show less NMA than novices. This is the standard summary of Repp (2005) and Repp & Su (2013, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, "Sensorimotor synchronization: A review of recent research (2006-2012)"). (snippet-level) — [Repp & Su 2013 record](https://www.altmetric.com/details/1262980); [Advances in Cognitive Psychology / DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/dc3f29b3977e4adf8dfe9aa998eb40de)
- **Subjective vs. objective synchrony.** NMA is "tens of milliseconds". People rarely notice it, and nonmusicians feel they are "tapping late" when they remove it. So the points of objective and subjective synchrony differ. Less negative asynchrony goes with lower tapping variability. — [Tapping ahead of time (BSL)](https://thim.mijn.bsl.nl/tapping-ahead-of-time-its-association-with-timing-variability/15893826); [Sci. Rep. 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-024-68326-6)
- **Musicians vs. nonmusicians.** Nonmusicians show larger NMA and more variability than musicians. Aschersleben (2002) is cited for "elite musicians had smaller NA". — [Psychological Research 2018 (s00426-018-1043-2)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00426-018-1043-2); [Ono et al. 2015, Front. Hum. Neurosci.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00154/full)
- **NMA depends on the task.** In Ono, Nakamura & Maess (2015), musicians following a conductor's gestures at a fast constant tempo showed a "small positive asynchrony (<50 ms)", while nonmusicians were negative. With a metronome, musicians and nonmusicians did not differ significantly. — [Ono et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00154/full)
- **Vocal vs. finger effectors.** Vocalisations are hypothesised to show *less* negative asynchrony than finger taps. The reasoning is that a vocal response is fed back in the same (auditory) modality as the pacer, whereas a tap requires comparing touch with sound. Finger tapping was more accurate than syllable repetition at the slowest tempo, musicians were more accurate than non-musicians, and asynchrony grew with age. (snippet-level; primary PDF not readable) — [Univ. Konstanz KOPS paper](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/783996b2-6c04-4316-8b36-5bc2f13d64b3/content); [ICPhS 2019 paper 741](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_741.pdf)
- **Which part of a sung syllable is "on the beat" (P-centre).** When syllables are produced in time with a metronome, the vowel onset acts as the attractor point (the P-centre). The P-centre usually lies near the consonant-to-vowel transition, not at the consonant onset. In one study the beat sat near the onset of the first consonant, and with repetition the consonant onset moved closer to the beat while the vowel onset moved further away. Taps synchronised to speech anchor consistently on vowel onsets. — [Barbosa et al., Interspeech 2005](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2005/barbosa05_interspeech.pdf); [Rathcke et al., "How to hit that beat"](https://pure.hud.ac.uk/en/publications/how-to-hit-that-beat-testing-acoustic-anchors-of-rhythmic-movemen/)
- **Singing duos: D'Amario, Daffern & Bailes (2018, Front. Psychol. 9:1208).**
  - Setup: 12 duos (24 university singers, mean age 20.9), a homophonic two-part piece, metronome at 100 bpm during rehearsal. Onsets were detected with TIMEX, which was 78% accurate within ±50 ms of expert annotation.
  - Precision (mean absolute asynchrony between singers): **58.99 ms (SD 11.13)**.
  - Consistency (SD of asynchronies): **67.06 ms (SD 11.85)**.
  - Signed bias (median signed asynchrony): **-4.06 ms (IQR 4.38)**.
  - With visual contact the mean absolute asynchrony was 56.0 ms; without it, 60.1 ms.
  - [D'Amario et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01208/full)
- **Singing duos and quintet, measured with electrolaryngography: D'Amario et al. (2019).**
  - Semi-professional duos: mean asynchrony **57 ms (SD 44.9)** with visual contact and **95 ms (SD 72.5)** without.
  - Advanced quintet: average asynchronies **>49 ms**, improving by up to about 10 ms across rehearsals.
  - [D'Amario et al. 2019, PMC6561579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561579)
- **Instrumental ensemble reference values.**
  - Rasch (1979) found a mean asynchrony of **36 ms** between nominally simultaneous tones in trios.
  - Between-player asynchrony SD: string trio **49 ms** at 79 bpm; wind ensemble **32 ms** at 88 bpm; string quartets **24-28 ms** at 157 bpm.
  - Professional ensembles playing at 40-130 bpm show variability of "tens of ms", which shrinks as tempo rises.
  - [D'Amario et al. 2018 review section](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01208/full); [Hirsh / ensemble summary, Acoust. Sci. Tech.](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast/22/3/22_3_239/_article)

### Inferences
- A sung onset is not a single sharp event. The perceived "beat point" is near the vowel onset, which comes tens of ms after the consonant onset. So whichever onset definition you pick (consonant burst, vowel onset, energy peak) adds a systematic bias of its own, which depends on the phonemes sung. That bias adds to the singer's NMA.
- Plausible voice-vs-beat bias for a single trained singer probably lies somewhere between about -50 and +30 ms. Tapping NMA (-20 to -80 ms) is likely an upper bound on anticipation, because vocal NMA is hypothesised to be smaller, and ensemble signed biases are only a few ms. Per-note scatter is large: SD around 30-70 ms.

### Gaps
- No primary study was found that reports the mean ± SD asynchrony of a solo singer against a *recorded backing track or karaoke* (as opposed to a metronome or another live singer). The specific vocal-vs-tapping NMA numbers from the Konstanz paper could not be extracted because the PDF was not text-readable here.
- The exact Repp (2005) figures for musicians vs. nonmusicians (often quoted from memory as about -10 to -30 ms vs. about -50 ms) could not be confirmed from a fetched primary text. Treat them as unverified.

## Q2. Expressive timing in popular singing: "laid-back" / "behind the beat" vocals

### Takeaway
Large-scale analysis of commercial recordings finds that singers on average slightly *anticipate* strong beats. The shifts are small, about a few hundredths of a beat or bar, which is typically on the order of 5-30 ms. Systematic late placement is genre-specific: R&B onsets on the main subdivision arrive about a 32nd note late, which is roughly 60 ms at 120 bpm. Microtiming varies by beat position, genre, era and song section, so it is not a single per-song constant.

### Cited Findings
- **Georgieva et al. (2026, TISMIR), "The Vocal Eras Tour: Microtiming Trends Across Decades and Genres".**
  - Data and methods: 88,357 vocal tracks from the Million Song Dataset (1965-2010), 30-60 s excerpts. Beats come from the "Beat This!" tracker (F = 0.975) and onsets from SuperFlux (F = 0.742 on 90 annotated vocal stems). Only duple meter was analysed.
  - Strong beats 1 and 3: estimated marginal mean deviations of **-0.0123 and -0.0110** (early). Beats 2 and 4: **-0.0028 and -0.0021**.
  - 16th-note subdivisions: N = **+0.0122** and + = **+0.0170** (late); E and A are about 0.
  - Wording quoted from the paper: "Singers tend to anticipate the beat, especially on beats 1 and 3", and these entries "have shifted closer to metrical targets over time".
  - [TISMIR article 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)
- **Genre differences in the same study.** Genre effects are significant at every beat position (e.g. beat 1: F(9, 82987) = 293.6). Pop and Country are slightly early. R&B, Latin and Metal arrive about a 32nd note late, and the most extreme case is R&B on subdivision N, "a little over a 32nd note late". Rap has the highest onset density (2.06 onsets per beat vs. 0.59-1.09 for other genres), and its deviations may cancel out symmetrically around the grid. — [TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)
- **Trend over time and spread.** Beat-level deviations drift toward the grid over 1965-2010, possibly because of click tracks and digital editing. The spread was estimated by fitting a normal distribution to the onsets in each subdivision. — [TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)
- **Units are ambiguous.** The paper says deviations are beat-normalised and that onsets are rounded to 0, 0.25, 0.5 or 0.75 of the beat. It also says "a microtiming difference of ±0.06 is equivalent to approximately one 16th note", which fits fractions of a *bar*: 1/16 = 0.0625. — [TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)
- **Laid-back drumming.** Drummers told to play "laid-back" against a click at 96 bpm delayed snare backbeats by **17.4 ms** on average. — [Carter & von Appen, Theory & Practice](https://tnp.mtsnys.org/vol49-50/carter_von_appen)
- **How large a timing shift is noticeable.** The just-noticeable difference for timing discrepancies is **2.5% of the beat** for trained musicians and **4.4%** for untrained listeners. That is about 12.5 ms and 22 ms at 120 bpm. — [Carter & von Appen](https://tnp.mtsnys.org/assets/articles/carter-von-appen/carter-von-appen.pdf)
- **Microtiming changes across song sections.** Hosken (IASPM 2020), "Tight verses and loose choruses", reports that microtiming "pocket" changes across pop song forms. The verse/chorus distinction comes from the title only; values were not retrieved. — [Hosken, IASPM 2020](https://london-calling-iaspm2020.com/session-3-theme-analysing-popular-music/tight-verses-and-loose-choruses-the-shaping-of-the-metric-pocket-across-pop-forms)
- **Recording practice affects timing variability.** Recordings with shared room acoustics and bleed between instruments show *lower* timing variability than highly isolated recordings, and overdubbing is associated with *higher* variability. (Abstract only; no ms values.) — [Tsioutas & Xylomenos 2026, arXiv 2609.13881](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13881)
- **Jazz.** Benadon (2007, Music Theory Online) studies jazz soloists' beat-relative microtiming. The values were not extracted. — [Benadon, MTO 13.1](https://mtosmt.org/classic/mto.07.13.1/mto.07.13.1.benadon.html)

### Inferences
- Converted to ms, the population-average vocal bias is small. If the units are fractions of a bar, -0.012 bar at 120 bpm (2 s bar) is about **-25 ms**. If they are fractions of a beat, it is about **-6 ms**. The R&B "32nd note late" is about **+62 ms at 120 bpm** and about **+94 ms at 80 bpm**.
- So genuine expressive vocal bias can reach about 60-100 ms late in some genres and tempos. That overlaps the low end of the 50-300 ms latency range of interest. Offsets above about 100-150 ms are hard to explain as style.
- The bias depends on beat position and section (e.g. early on beats 1/3, late on subdivisions). A pure technical latency shifts *all* positions equally. This is a weak handle for separating the two (see Q5).

### Gaps
- No per-song or per-phrase SD values in ms were retrieved from the TISMIR paper. It reports genre/position means and states that normal fits were used, but the fitted SDs were not given in the text fetched.
- No study was found that measures how consistent a singer's lateness is *within* a song (per-phrase vs. constant) on commercial ballad vocals.

## Q3. Reaction to auditory feedback latency: delayed auditory feedback (DAF), monitoring latency, and networked music performance (NMP)

### Takeaway
Singers and players lock to what they *hear*. Small one-way delays of about 10-30 ms are tolerated, and delays above about 20-30 ms make ensembles slow down. Delaying a performer's own voice by about 200 ms or more seriously disrupts timing. Musicians can consciously work out strategies for delay, but no source found shows them automatically pre-compensating for an unknown constant offset. So a recording made with headphone and interface latency will carry that latency into the voice-vs-backing timing.

### Cited Findings
- **Chafe & Gurevich (2004), hand-clapping over delay.**
  - Setup: pairs of musicians in isolated rooms, monitoring each other over headphones, with the one-way delay varied.
  - Delays **<11.5 ms** produced a modest *speed-up*: 74% of trials accelerated.
  - Delays of **≥14 ms** produced a slow-down: 85% of trials decelerated.
  - Deceleration grew roughly linearly with delay, with no sharp breakdown point. Performance was still feasible at about 20 ms one-way, roughly the San Francisco-Denver network delay.
  - [Chafe et al., CCRMA](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~cc/pub/html/ntd.html); [Chafe AES paper](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~cc/vox/aesFinal.pdf); [Stanford mediaX](https://mediax.stanford.edu/research-projects/lat-chafe)
- **Ensemble Performance Threshold (EPT).** The EPT is the one-way delay below which synchronisation stays achievable, put at **20-30 ms** in early NMP studies. — [Rottondi, Chafe, Allocchio & Sarti 2016, IEEE Access](https://doaj.org/article/b6289c0973fd4f6d9543aaef43acdb70); [preprint](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2723336/1/nmp.pdf)
- **Other latency limits.** At 20-60 ms, performance degrades with increasing tempo deceleration. One user study put the performance threshold around **50 ms**, with musicians rating about **65 ms** as the maximum acceptable delay. Musicians who were aware of the delay devised strategies to avoid slowing down, or let a controlled slowdown stabilise. A "delayed feedback compensation" approach deliberately inserts a user-set delay into the musician's own monitoring. — [NMP literature summary, e.g. Comanducci (Politecnico di Milano)](https://re.public.polimi.it/retrieve/e7590536-5816-48ba-b497-af870edfb86a/11311-1183174_Comanducci.pdf); [Rottondi et al. 2016](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2723336/1/nmp.pdf)
- **Live-sound monitoring.** Lester & Boley (2007, AES Conv. Paper 7198): acceptable monitor latency ranged from **42 ms down to <1.4 ms**, depending on instrument and monitor type. In-ear monitors tolerate less latency than wedges, because the singer's own direct or bone-conducted sound combs with the delayed signal. — [AES e-library 14256](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=14256)
- **Delayed auditory feedback (DAF).**
  - Disruption is strongest around **200 ms**. It depends on the loudness of the delayed signal and the delay length, and requires **≥0.2 s** to be strong.
  - In singing, the most disruptive delays are about one average syllable duration.
  - Altering feedback *timing* disrupts production timing, whereas altering feedback *content* (pitch order) disrupts accuracy.
  - [Pfordresher & Varco 2010 ICMPC](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Varco_2010_ICMPC.pdf); [Pruitt & Pfordresher 2015 JEPHPP](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pruitt&Pfordresher_2015_JEPHPP.pdf); [Sci. Rep. 2015 srep08800](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep08800)
- **Instrument attack time matters in NMP.** Perceptual attack times affect synchronisation in networked performance, so slow-attack sounds such as sung legato give fuzzier timing cues. — [Barbosa & Cordeiro 2011](https://repository.uwl.ac.uk/id/eprint/5266/1/Barbosa,%20Cordeiro%20-%202011%20-%20The%20Influence%20of%20Perceptual%20Attack%20Times%20in%20Networked%20Music%20Performance.pdf)

### Inferences
These apply the cited behaviour (people align to what they hear) to a live-stream signal chain.
- **Notation.**
  - d_out = latency of the backing's playback path to the headphones.
  - d_in = latency of the mic and interface capture path.
  - The stream mixes the backing as sent, before the playback delay, with the captured voice.
- **Case A: the singer hears their own voice acoustically or by bone conduction, with no software monitoring.**
  - The singer aligns their produced voice with the backing as heard, which is late by d_out.
  - The captured voice is then late by a further d_in.
  - Result: the voice in the mix lags by about **d_out + d_in** (+ the singer's own NMA/expressive bias).
  - The singer cannot hear d_in, so they cannot correct for it.
- **Case B: the singer monitors their voice through the delayed software path.**
  - If d_in + d_out exceeds about 20-40 ms, the singer hears their own voice late relative to their actual voice.
  - DAF studies predict disruption, especially toward 200 ms or more.
  - The singer may sing slightly ahead to align the heard voice with the heard backing. That would partly cancel d_in in the mix, but evidence of reliable automatic compensation was not found.
- **Expected size.** Typical consumer setups (Bluetooth headphones, OS audio buffers, browser/WebRTC capture) give d_out + d_in from tens to hundreds of ms. This would produce exactly the 50-300 ms constant offsets in question. Singers would most likely not notice their own offset, because they perceive themselves as in time with what they hear.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found of *singers* specifically (as opposed to clappers or instrumentalists) under a fixed backing-track delay that reports whether their produced onsets shift by the delay amount.
- No quantitative data were found on how fast musicians adapt to a constant monitoring latency, e.g. a sensorimotor recalibration time course.

## Q4. Perception: thresholds for noticing voice/backing misalignment (audio-audio), plus audio-visual lip-sync context

### Takeaway
Between two sounds, temporal order can be detected at about 15-20 ms by trained listeners. In real ensemble music, listeners detect differences in asynchrony somewhere between about 10 and 38 ms, with reported thresholds of about 30 ms for piano tones and about 40 ms for complex sounds. Offsets of 50-300 ms between voice and backing are therefore well above perceptual thresholds and would sound "off". For audio-video lip sync, broadcast limits are tighter for audio leading (+40/+45 ms) than for audio lagging (-60 to -125 ms).

### Cited Findings
- **Temporal order (Hirsh).** The limit for perceiving the temporal order of two sounds is about **≥20 ms** (Hirsh). For highly trained listeners, the threshold for the order of two asynchronous tones is **15-20 ms**, largely independent of frequency. Onset asynchrony of about **30 ms** affects perception even when listeners cannot detect it. — [Hirsh retrospective, Thieme 2004](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-2004-832856?lang=en); [Acoust. Sci. Tech. 22(3)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast/22/3/22_3_239/_article)
- **Singing ensembles (D'Amario et al. 2019).** Listeners perceived a **38 ms** difference in asynchrony between duo conditions. They did *not* perceive about **10 ms** differences across quintet rehearsals, regardless of musical expertise. The authors place the threshold "somewhere between 10 and 38 ms" and cite prior thresholds of about **40 ms** for complex sounds and about **30 ms** for acoustic piano tones. — [PMC6561579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561579)
- **Ensemble asynchrony in practice.** Asynchronies in small ensembles are typically **30-50 ms**, and perceived asynchrony within ensembles ranges from **20 to 50 ms**. Many computational analyses use **50 ms** as their tolerance window. Humans can recognise performance differences from timing changes as small as 20 ms. — [ViennaTalk 2015 submission 55](https://viennatalk2015.mdw.ac.at/proceedings/ViennaTalk2015_submission_55.pdf); [AUDITORY list thread 2019](https://auditory.org/mhonarc/2019/msg00231.html)
- **Singing against a metronome click.** Asynchronies of **40 ms** were judged better than 20 ms ones. A **20 ms** asynchrony was identified more accurately when the metronome *followed* the sung tone than when it preceded it, so the threshold is asymmetric. (snippet-level) — [Production Biases and Perception of Synchrony](https://cd-create.org/node/316)
- **Lip sync, ITU-R BT.1359 (1998).**
  - Detectability: **+45 ms** (audio leads) to **-125 ms** (audio lags).
  - Acceptability: **+90 ms** to **-185/-190 ms**.
  - Based on subjective tests in Japan, Switzerland and Australia.
  - [ITU-R BT.1359-0 PDF](https://itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-0-199802-S!!PDF-E.pdf); [Wikipedia: Audio-to-video synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization)
- **Lip sync, EBU R37.** End-to-end limits of **+40 ms / -60 ms** (audio before / after video), with each processing stage held within +5 / -15 ms. — [Wikipedia: Audio-to-video synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization); [TV Technology](https://www.tvtechnology.com/opinions/av-synchronization-how-bad-is-bad)

### Inferences
- For voice against backing, a correction target of **≤20-30 ms** residual error is perceptually sufficient. Natural singer scatter (SD about 40-70 ms per note) already exceeds this, but listeners judge the overall alignment, not single notes.
- Audio-audio sensitivity (about 20-40 ms) is stricter than lip-sync tolerance (about 45-125 ms). So a voice that lags the backing by 50-300 ms is clearly audible as "late singing" or "echo-like" at the upper end.

### Gaps
- No study was found that gives a threshold specifically for a constant lag of a *lead vocal against a full backing mix* (as opposed to tone pairs, piano, or singer-singer). The estimate of about 30-40 ms is extrapolated.

## Q5. Statistical identifiability: separating a constant technical latency from the singer's expressive/NMA bias

### Takeaway
From voice-vs-beat timing alone, only the **sum** of technical latency and the singer's mean bias is identifiable. They are perfectly confounded in a single mean. They can be separated only through *priors* on the plausible bias range, or through structure that latency cannot produce, such as beat-position-dependent bias, or the onset-definition bias from consonant vs. vowel onsets. Offsets that are near multiples of a beat subdivision are additionally ambiguous (aliasing). Very little literature addresses this estimation problem directly.

### Cited Findings
- **Analysis tools exist but do not separate the two.** Onset-asynchrony analysis gives accuracy (mean) and precision (stability) of synchronisation between performers. The `onsetsync` R package, for example, takes per-instrument onsets aligned to labelled beats. These tools describe asynchronies; they do not separate their causes. — [Durham repository, onset-asynchrony analysis](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/2119627); [onsetsync](https://rdrr.io/github/tuomaseerola/onsetsynch/)
- **Asynchronies are not always normal.** Assuming normally distributed asynchronies can considerably *underestimate* both the mean offset and the variability. Distributions tend to be bimodal and left-skewed when the inter-stimulus interval exceeds 2 s. A robust estimator (median or mixture model) is therefore preferable. — [Behav. Res. Methods s13428-015-0591-2](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13428-015-0591-2)
- **Size of individual differences in mean bias.** Individual NMA differs between people (musicians vs. nonmusicians, effector, age) and by task (metronome vs. conductor, where musicians were even positive). Mean signed bias in singer-singer coordination is about -4 ms (IQR 4.4 ms) in the duo study. — [D'Amario et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01208/full); [Ono et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00154/full)
- **Expressive bias in recordings.** Genre-level vocal bias in commercial recordings ranges from slightly early (Pop/Country) to about a 32nd note late (R&B, Latin, Metal), and differs by beat position. — [TISMIR 278](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/278)
- No paper was found that estimates an unknown constant recording or monitoring latency *from musical timing statistics alone* (e.g. "latency estimation from beat-relative onset distribution"). NMP papers measure latency directly from the network or audio loopback rather than inferring it from performance. — [Rottondi et al. 2016](https://iris.polito.it/bitstream/11583/2723336/1/nmp.pdf)

### Inferences
These are mathematical and design reasoning, not sourced findings.
- **The core model.** For note i, the observed voice-minus-beat offset is o_i = L + b + e_i, where:
  - L = constant technical latency;
  - b = the singer's mean bias (NMA + style + onset-detector bias);
  - e_i = per-note scatter.
- **Precision is not the bottleneck; the prior on b is.**
  - With per-note SD σ about 40-70 ms (from the duo data) and N notes, the standard error of L + b is σ/√N. For σ = 50 and N = 100, that is about 5 ms.
  - L itself is determined only up to the prior on b. A reasonable prior is b ~ N(-10 ms, 25-30 ms), giving a 95% band of about -60 to +40 ms. That covers tapping NMA, vocal P-centre bias, and the genre microtiming above.
  - So any estimate of L + b outside about [-60, +100] ms is very unlikely to be expressive. At 80-120 bpm the R&B "32nd late" case reaches +60 to +95 ms, which sets the upper edge.
  - **Offsets of 100-300 ms can therefore be attributed to latency with high confidence. Offsets of 50-100 ms are ambiguous with laid-back styles.**
- **Separation by structure.**
  - A technical latency shifts all beat positions, onsets and note types by the same L.
  - Expressive timing differs between beat positions: early on beats 1 and 3, near zero on beats 2 and 4, late on subdivisions.
  - Expressive timing also changes over the song, with tighter verses and looser choruses.
  - So the *minimum-magnitude* or *best-aligned* subset of onsets (e.g. strong-beat entries, or consonant-onset vs. vowel-onset landmarks) may constrain L better than the overall mean. This remains a heuristic; no source validates it.
- **Beat-period aliasing.**
  - Because singing is quasi-periodic, the distribution of onset phases relative to the beat is periodic in the beat period T, and its subdivisions (T/2, T/4) carry much of the onset mass.
  - A latency L and a latency L ± T/4 can give similar phase histograms, especially in rhythmically dense styles.
  - Subdivision sizes by tempo:

    | Tempo | 16th note | 8th note | Beat |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 120 bpm | 125 ms | 250 ms | 500 ms |
    | 90 bpm | 167 ms | 333 ms | 667 ms |

  - So within a 50-300 ms search range there is usually at least one subdivision alias.
  - Ways to break the aliasing:
    - lyric- or note-level alignment, e.g. comparing to a reference melody or score;
    - phrase starts after rests (downbeat entries);
    - priors that favour the smallest |L + b|, given the known small vocal bias.
- **Style and spread.**
  - Rap has the highest onset density (2.06 per beat) and deviations distributed symmetrically around the grid. It gives sharp, many-onset phase histograms: better precision, but stronger subdivision aliasing.
  - Legato ballads have slow vocal attacks (cf. the perceptual-attack-time effects in NMP), lower onset density, and more onset-detection error (TIMEX was 78% accurate within ±50 ms; SuperFlux F = 0.742 on vocals). They give wider scatter and fewer, noisier landmarks, but less aliasing.

### Gaps
- No published estimator or validation study was found for "latency from musical timing". Within this scope's searches, this appears to be an open problem.
- No direct measurements were found of beat-relative vocal-onset SD by style, such as ballad vs. up-tempo, in ms. The TISMIR study fitted per-subdivision SDs, but they were not retrievable here.
